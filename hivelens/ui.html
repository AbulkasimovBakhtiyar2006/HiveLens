A field reference

Know which mite you’re looking for.

Different parasites need different checks. Location is a clue, not a diagnosis.

What HiveLens can assess

Our photo workflow counts Varroa-like mites on sticky boards. The benchmark covers those boards only. It does not identify mite species, rule out infestation, or detect tracheal mites. Keep the board’s exposure time with every count.

Board-count workflow01

Varroa destructor

The main target of this project

Where
Widespread internationally in managed honey bees. Check your local authority for current regional status.
In the hive
On adult bees and in brood cells. A small, flattened, reddish-brown mite.
What to check
Count fallen mites on a sticky board over a known interval. A count per day is not the same as mites per 100 bees from a wash.
DAFF: Varroa biology ↗
Species identification unsupported02

Varroa jacobsoni

A related species with a different host history

Where
Associated with Asian honey bees; a host shift to western honey bees was documented in Papua New Guinea in 2008.
In the hive
Another Varroa species. A photo count cannot reliably distinguish it from V. destructor.
What to check
Ask a bee-health specialist for species confirmation when an unusual infestation is suspected.
DAFF: species and host shift ↗
Detection unsupported03

Tropilaelaps mites

T. mercedesae and T. clareae

Where
Historically associated with Asia. Reports beyond the traditional range include the Caucasus; distribution is changing.
In the hive
External parasites of brood. A Varroa-specific image filter is not a suitable test for these mites.
What to check
Seek specialist identification and follow local reporting guidance. A negative HiveLens result cannot exclude them.
EU reference laboratory: spread ↗
Microscopy required04

Acarapis woodi

The honey bee tracheal mite

Where
Reported in multiple beekeeping regions; consult local bee-health surveillance for country status.
In the hive
A microscopic parasite inside the respiratory tubes of adult bees.
What to check
Diagnosis requires examination of the tracheae. It cannot be made from a normal frame or sticky-board photograph.
National Bee Unit: tracheal mites ↗
Read the evidence before acting.

Wax, pollen and debris can resemble mites. Inspect the marked-up image and rejected candidates. Regional thresholds depend on the sampling method, season and colony; this guide does not prescribe treatments.

      How it works

      Models look at the photos, plain code does the measuring, and the report can only say what the measurements support.

      1. 1sam-3.1

        Find

        The board photo is cut into 600 px tiles, and unclear dark spots are checked again at 300 px. On frames, SAM outlines the comb and every bee.

      2. 2muse-spark-1.3

        Review

        Spots SAM does not recognise go onto numbered sheets of 36, and Muse Spark marks the mites. Frame tiles are labelled honey, brood, larvae, pollen or empty.

      3. 3opencv, numpy

        Measure

        Every candidate must look like a mite: the right size, reddish-brown, a smooth oval. Rules then set the status from the numbers.

      4. 4tool calls

        Report

        Muse Spark states what it expects before it sees the numbers. Each claim is checked, and findings without a verified claim are dropped.

      Limits

      Comb reading has no benchmark yet. We found no openly licensed frame photos with expert labels, so frame results were checked by eye.

      The default thresholds in hivelens/config.py are 3 mites a day to watch and 10 to act. Adjust them for your season and colony size.

      Demo hives are marked as demo data everywhere, so they never mix with real measurements.
